Consent and informational privacy in the Supreme Court of India, 2021 to 2026

Thirteen reasoned judgments, decided between September 2021 and August 2026, on when a person's consent or control over their body and information wins against the State, a spouse, a family member or the public's right to know. Set against the road from Puttaswamy to the DPDP Act.

    Before the DPDP Act was passed, the privacy or consent argument won all four judgments. Afterwards the record is closer (5 wins, 4 losses), but none of the losses is a win for State power. Each came where privacy met another person's right: voters (electoral bonds), a minor victim (POCSO), a spouse proving cruelty, and a son seeking his paternity. The compelled-tests strand shows the change most clearly. DNA tests of children went from near-barred (Aparna Firodia, 2023), to an 'eminent need' test (Ivan Rathinam, Jan 2025), to proportionality with a nexus to the offence (R. Rajendran, Nov 2025), to a test actually being ordered once those conditions were met (Chaturbhuj Pradhan, 2026).

    Privacy or consent prevailed in 9 of 13 judgments. Compelled tests are the strongest strand (4 of 5): forced narco-analysis is never allowed, and DNA tests need an "eminent need". Bodily autonomy follows (3 of 4), with the end-of-life cases moving towards making consent workable for patients who cannot give it. Informational privacy is evenly split, because two of its four cases set privacy against another person's right to information or to prove a case.

    Individuals brought 10 of the 13 cases and won 10. The State's only win, in the POCSO adolescents case, rested on protecting a child, not on any power to intrude. Both cases brought by associations were won by them, in opposite directions: a doctors' body (the Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine) got the living-will rules eased in Common Cause, while ADR used the right to know to defeat a privacy claim.

    The doctrinal trend

    Consent as a shield against State coercion is close to absolute. In Amlesh Kumar (2025) the Court reaffirmed Selvi: a forced narco test is never permissible, and even a voluntary one may be sought only at the defence stage with the court's permission. Frank Vitus (2024) applied the same logic to location data, striking down a bail condition that let police track an accused. In the Pegasus case (2021), a bare plea of national security did not stop an inquiry.

    Compelled DNA testing went from a near-bar to a structured test. Aparna Firodia (2023, paras 16–24) held that a child's genetic information is private and DNA tests cannot be ordered routinely. Ivan Rathinam (Jan 2025, paras 46–47) turned this into two hurdles: existing evidence must be insufficient, and the balance of interests must favour the test. R. Rajendran (Nov 2025, paras 45–48, 52–54) added Puttaswamy proportionality and a nexus to the offence. Chaturbhuj Pradhan (2026, paras 5.3–7) relied on Aparna and Ivan Rathinam and then ordered a test, because paternity was the very issue in the suit. Privacy here is weighed, not assumed, and the line of authority builds on itself case by case.

    Privacy against other people's rights is a balance, not a trump. In the electoral bonds case it was the State that invoked donor privacy, and it lost to voters' right to information. In Vibhor Garg (2025) a spouse's privacy gave way to the other spouse's right to prove cruelty with secret recordings.

    Consent is read in context. X v. Principal Secretary (2022) protected a minor's identity even though the law treats her consent as irrelevant. In Re: Right to Privacy of Adolescents (2024) refused to let "consent" undo a POCSO conviction. Common Cause (2023) simplified the living-will procedure so that advance directives can work in practice, and Harish Rana (2026) applied that framework to let medical boards and family supply consent for a patient who could not give it.

    Why this matters for the DPDP Act and AI. The Act makes consent the default ground for processing personal data, but lets the State and others process data without it as "legitimate uses". The case law suggests courts will police consent strictly where the State intrudes on a person directly, and will apply a proportionality test where other rights compete. Whether that strictness extends to large-scale data collection, such as data used to train AI, is untested. None of the thirteen judgments turns on the DPDP Act.

    Method

    Scope

    Supreme Court of India; reasoned judgments decided between 01-09-2021 and 31-08-2026. Orders without reasons and notices are excluded. A judgment is included only if a privacy or consent argument was central to what the Court decided, not merely raised in passing.

    Sources

    Judgments were found by searching Indian Kanoon, LiveLaw and Supreme Court Observer for “right to privacy”, “informational privacy”, “consent”, “narco-analysis”, “DNA test” and “Article 21”, then read and coded from the official copy on the Supreme Court's own website (sci.gov.in or Digital SCR), linked from every entry. Secondary sources were used only to find cases, never as the source of a citation. Paragraph pinpoints in the "Main provision" column refer to the official copy.

    Coding

    Each judgment is coded in the course's Dataset template (data.xlsx / data.csv). Two further labels are the team's own: the strand, and whether the privacy or consent argument prevailed. Where the State itself invoked privacy (electoral bonds), "yielded" means the privacy claim lost, even though the petitioners won.

    Limits

    Thirteen judgments from one court cannot establish a statistical trend. The set covers the Court's main reasoned rulings where privacy or consent decided the case. It is not every judgment that mentions privacy, and a wider search would add many cases where privacy was argued only in passing. The strand labels and the "did privacy prevail" coding are the team's own judgment calls. The Supreme Court mostly hears appeals, so this set says nothing about High Courts or the Data Protection Board.
